Topic

Series solutions

Many equations of physics have no solution in elementary functions: Airy's, Bessel's, Legendre's. Their solutions are defined by power series. seriesSolution builds those series for \(y'' + p(x)\,y' + q(x)\,y = 0\) about a point, with exact rational coefficients, and says what kind of point it is.

seriesSolution({ q: "-x", order: 7 });            // Airy: y'' = x y
// kind "ordinary", solutions "1 + 1/6*x^3 + 1/180*x^6 + …" and "x + 1/12*x^4 + 1/504*x^7 + …"

seriesSolution({ p: "1/x", q: "(x^2 - 1/4)/x^2" });   // Bessel of order 1/2
// kind "regular singular", indicial "r^2 - 1/4 = 0", exponents [1/2, -1/2], two series

Ordinary points

If \(p\) and \(q\) are analytic at \(x_0\), every solution is a power series \(\sum a_n (x - x_0)^n\) there, and it converges at least as far as the nearest singularity of \(p\) or \(q\) in the complex plane. Substituting the series and matching powers gives a recurrence that determines every \(a_n\) from \(a_0\) and \(a_1\):

\[ (n+2)(n+1)\,a_{n+2} = -\sum_{k=0}^{n} \bigl( p_k\,(n-k+1)\,a_{n-k+1} + q_k\,a_{n-k} \bigr). \]

The two solutions returned are the ones with \((y, y')(x_0) = (1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\). radius estimates the distance to the nearest singularity.

Regular singular points and Frobenius

If \(p\) has at most a simple pole and \(q\) at most a double pole at \(x_0\), the point is a regular singular point. Write \(x p(x) = \sum P_k x^k\) and \(x^2 q(x) = \sum Q_k x^k\) (taking \(x_0 = 0\)). Frobenius' method looks for \(y = x^r \sum a_n x^n\); the lowest power gives the indicial equation

\[ r(r - 1) + P_0\,r + Q_0 = 0, \]

and for each root \(r\) the recurrence

\[ \bigl[(n+r)(n+r-1) + P_0 (n+r) + Q_0\bigr]\,a_n = -\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(P_k (n-k+r) + Q_k\bigr)\,a_{n-k}. \]

The larger root always gives a series. The smaller root gives a second one unless the roots are equal or differ by a whole number, in which case the bracket on the left can vanish for some \(n\); then the second solution contains \(\log x\), and notes says so. (When the right side happens to vanish too, the series exists after all, as for Bessel's equation of order one half.)

A point where the poles are worse is an irregular singular point, and the method does not apply.

Checking a series

The coefficients are exact, and a partial sum is a polynomial whose value at a rational point is an exact rational. Comparing it with the validated solution of the same initial value problem shows the true truncation error, as in the box below.
