Topic

Series solutions

Many equations of physics have no solution in elementary functions: Airy's, Bessel's, Legendre's. Their solutions are defined by power series. seriesSolution builds those series for \(y'' + p(x)\,y' + q(x)\,y = 0\) about a point, with exact rational coefficients, and says what kind of point it is.

seriesSolution({ q: "-x", order: 7 });            // Airy: y'' = x y
// kind "ordinary", solutions "1 + 1/6*x^3 + 1/180*x^6 + …" and "x + 1/12*x^4 + 1/504*x^7 + …"

seriesSolution({ p: "1/x", q: "(x^2 - 1/4)/x^2" });   // Bessel of order 1/2
// kind "regular singular", indicial "r^2 - 1/4 = 0", exponents [1/2, -1/2], two series

Ordinary points

If \(p\) and \(q\) are analytic at \(x_0\), every solution is a power series \(\sum a_n (x - x_0)^n\) there, and it converges at least as far as the nearest singularity of \(p\) or \(q\) in the complex plane. Substituting the series and matching powers gives a recurrence that determines every \(a_n\) from \(a_0\) and \(a_1\):

\[ (n+2)(n+1)\,a_{n+2} = -\sum_{k=0}^{n} \bigl( p_k\,(n-k+1)\,a_{n-k+1} + q_k\,a_{n-k} \bigr). \]

The two solutions returned are the ones with \((y, y')(x_0) = (1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\). radius estimates the distance to the nearest singularity.

Regular singular points and Frobenius

If \(p\) has at most a simple pole and \(q\) at most a double pole at \(x_0\), the point is a regular singular point. Write \(x p(x) = \sum P_k x^k\) and \(x^2 q(x) = \sum Q_k x^k\) (taking \(x_0 = 0\)). Frobenius' method looks for \(y = x^r \sum a_n x^n\); the lowest power gives the indicial equation

\[ r(r - 1) + P_0\,r + Q_0 = 0, \]

and for each root \(r\) the recurrence

\[ \bigl[(n+r)(n+r-1) + P_0 (n+r) + Q_0\bigr]\,a_n = -\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(P_k (n-k+r) + Q_k\bigr)\,a_{n-k}. \]

The larger root always gives a series. The smaller root gives a second one unless the roots are equal or differ by a whole number, in which case the bracket on the left can vanish for some \(n\); then the second solution contains \(\log x\), and notes says so. (When the right side happens to vanish too, the series exists after all, as for Bessel's equation of order one half.)

A point where the poles are worse is an irregular singular point, and the method does not apply.

A proven remainder

A truncated series is only useful with a bound on what was cut off. Each solution \(y = (x - x_0)^r g(x)\), \(g = \sum a_n (x - x_0)^n\), carries one: remainder(ρ) returns a rational \(B\) with

\[ \Bigl|\, g(x) - \sum_{n \le N} a_n (x - x_0)^n \Bigr| \le B \quad\text{for all complex } x \text{ with } |x - x_0| \le \rho , \]

for any \(\rho\) below provenRadius. enclose(x) evaluates the partial sum at a rational point and puts that bound on either side.

const cos = seriesSolution({ q: "1", order: 12 }).solutions[0];   // y'' + y = 0, y(0) = 1, y'(0) = 0
cos.remainder(1).bound;        // 447/1000000000000: the terms beyond x^12 are below 4.47e-10 on the unit disc
const e = cos.enclose("1/2");  // { partial, bound, low, high }
e.low.toDecimal(15), e.high.toDecimal(15);   // both 0.877582561890372…: cos(1/2) to 13 places, proven

The proof is the classical convergence argument made quantitative. Take \(x_0 = 0\). First, a radius: the reciprocals of the roots of the denominators of \(p\) and \(q\) are bounded by root squaring (Graeffe) and Fujiwara's bound, in exact arithmetic, giving \(R\) with no singular point in \(0 < |x| < R\); that is provenRadius, within about one percent of the true distance. Second, for a radius \(s < R\), numbers \(M_P, M_Q\) with \(|P_k| \le M_P/s^k\) and \(|Q_k| \le M_Q/s^k\) for all \(k \ge 1\): the first hundred or so coefficients are used exactly and the rest are bounded by a majorant of the form \((1 - x/R)^{-m}\). Third, the recurrence. With \(b_n = |a_n| s^n\) and \(c_j = M_P(|r| + j) + M_Q\) it gives

\[ n\,(n + \delta)\, b_n \le S_n := \sum_{j < n} c_j b_j, \qquad S_{n+1} \le S_n\,(1 + \varepsilon_n), \quad \varepsilon_n = \frac{c_n}{n(n + \delta)}, \]

where \(\delta = 2r - 1 + P_0\), because the bracket in the recurrence equals \(n(n + \delta)\). \(S_{N+1}\) is known exactly from the coefficients already computed, and \(\varepsilon_n\) decreases to 0. So for \(|x| \le \rho < s\) the tail \(\sum_{n > N} b_n (\rho/s)^n\) is at most a few explicit terms plus a geometric series, once \((1 + \varepsilon_n)\rho/s < 1\). Several values of \(s\) are tried and the smallest bound kept. Every step is rational arithmetic rounded upward.

The bound covers the series \(g\); the factor \((x - x_0)^r\) is explicit. Near the edge of the disc a bound still exists but is weak, as it must be: the series itself converges slowly there.

Checking a series

The coefficients are exact, and a partial sum is a polynomial whose value at a rational point is an exact rational. Comparing it with the validated solution of the same initial value problem shows the true truncation error next to the proven bound, as in the box below.

callreturns
seriesSolution({ p, q, about = 0, order = 8, x = "x" }){ about, kind, indicial, exponents, solutions, notes, radius, provenRadius }
solutions[i]{ exponent, coefficients, text, remainder, enclose }, coefficients exact
solutions[i].remainder(ρ){ radius, terms, bound, approx, text }: a proven bound for the terms left out, on the disc of radius ρ
solutions[i].enclose(x){ partial, bound, low, high, exponent }: the series factor at a rational x
