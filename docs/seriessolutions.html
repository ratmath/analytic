Series solutions

Many equations of physics have no solution in elementary functions: Airy's, Bessel's, Legendre's. Their solutions are defined by power series. seriesSolution builds those series for y′′+p(x) y′+q(x) y=0y'' + p(x)\,y' + q(x)\,y = 0y′′+p(x)y′+q(x)y=0 about a point, with exact rational coefficients, and says what kind of point it is.

seriesSolution({ q: "-x", order: 7 });            // Airy: y'' = x y
// kind "ordinary", solutions "1 + 1/6*x^3 + 1/180*x^6 + …" and "x + 1/12*x^4 + 1/504*x^7 + …"

seriesSolution({ p: "1/x", q: "(x^2 - 1/4)/x^2" });   // Bessel of order 1/2
// kind "regular singular", indicial "r^2 - 1/4 = 0", exponents [1/2, -1/2], two series

Regular singular points and Frobenius

If ppp has at most a simple pole and qqq at most a double pole at x0x_0x0​, the point is a regular singular point. Write xp(x)=∑Pkxkx p(x) = \sum P_k x^kxp(x)=∑Pk​xk and x2q(x)=∑Qkxkx^2 q(x) = \sum Q_k x^kx2q(x)=∑Qk​xk (taking x0=0x_0 = 0x0​=0). Frobenius' method looks for y=xr∑anxny = x^r \sum a_n x^ny=xr∑an​xn; the lowest power gives the indicial equation

r(r−1)+P0 r+Q0=0, r(r - 1) + P_0\,r + Q_0 = 0, r(r−1)+P0​r+Q0​=0,

and for each root rrr the recurrence

[(n+r)(n+r−1)+P0(n+r)+Q0] an=−∑k=1n(Pk(n−k+r)+Qk) an−k. \bigl[(n+r)(n+r-1) + P_0 (n+r) + Q_0\bigr]\,a_n = -\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(P_k (n-k+r) + Q_k\bigr)\,a_{n-k}. [(n+r)(n+r−1)+P0​(n+r)+Q0​]an​=−k=1∑n​(Pk​(n−k+r)+Qk​)an−k​.

The larger root always gives a series. The smaller root gives a second one unless the roots are equal or differ by a whole number, in which case the bracket on the left can vanish for some nnn; then the second solution contains log⁡x\log xlogx, and notes says so. (When the right side happens to vanish too, the series exists after all, as for Bessel's equation of order one half.)

A point where the poles are worse is an irregular singular point, and the method does not apply.
